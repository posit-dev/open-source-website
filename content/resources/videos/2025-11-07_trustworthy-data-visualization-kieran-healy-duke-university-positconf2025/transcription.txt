<h3>Technical analysis and reading stories into charts</h3>

<p><a data-t="231">One early stream of data</a> <a data-t="239">that people really wanted to tell a story about was this one, the Dow Jones Industrial Average</a> <a data-t="244">or averages. In the early days there was one for industry and one for the railroads and you can</a> <a data-t="248">just see kind of at the bottom there one for utilities too, which actually still exists.</a> <a data-t="253">The Dow Jones was a steady stream of data about the stock market as a whole and the desire to</a> <a data-t="258">transmit that data far and wide in a more or less continuous stream was one of the driving forces</a> <a data-t="263">behind the expansion of the telegraph, the development of modern communications technology</a> <a data-t="268">generally. Deep in the bones of your terminal, of your PowerShell, of your </a><a href="/software/positron/">Positron</a><a data-t="268"> or RStudio or</a> <a data-t="274">VS Code session, you will find the marks of the teletype and ticker tape machines that were built</a> <a data-t="280">to transmit price data. There was a reason that people really wanted that stream of data.</a> <a data-t="286">They wanted to make money and in some cases they wanted to make money by using graphs to see into</a> <a data-t="292">the future.</a></p>

<p><a data-t="292">This should really have been the original, I should have kept this title for the</a> <a data-t="296">talk. Charles Dow, the founder of both the Wall Street Journal and Dow Jones, was retrospectively</a> <a data-t="304">credited with the Dow Theory, which later became technical analysis. And technical analysis started</a> <a data-t="309">from price charts, which of course had to be drawn by hand, and combined it with a theory of</a> <a data-t="314">what investors in the market were thinking and then he used that to predict prices. And in practice,</a> <a data-t="320">the main technique was telling stories with graphs. The people who did this were the chart men,</a> <a data-t="325">and they were all men. The classic source is a book by Robert Edwards and John McGee called</a> <a data-t="331">Technical Analysis of Stock Trends. It came out in 1938 and it is still in print. It's now in its</a> <a data-t="337">11th edition, I think. This book by William Gilers from the late 1960s was put out by Standard and</a> <a data-t="342">Poor's. The idea was that if you looked at trends in stock prices, either for the market as a whole</a> <a data-t="348">or for individual stocks, you could spot recurring patterns and patterns that told you what might</a> <a data-t="353">happen next. Not infallibly, of course, but enough to give you an edge, to let you make an informed</a> <a data-t="359">decision to buy or sell.</a></p>

<p><a data-t="359">So you start with a high-low closed chart like this one, which is</a> <a data-t="364">from Edwards and McGee, and then you start looking for the telltale patterns. What patterns, exactly?</a> <a data-t="370">Well, it turns out there are a lot. Now anyone can see a basic upward or downward trend, but at this point</a> <a data-t="408">the theory starts to get a little richer. One idea was that after a period of steady growth, you would</a> <a data-t="413">often see investors pull back from a stock as they lost confidence in it, and hence the pullback</a> <a data-t="418">effect. So the trick would be to sell at the top of that third peak there. They had a whole family</a> <a data-t="423">of these patterns, each with a name, a distinctive visual signature, and a story associated with what</a> <a data-t="430">to do when you spotted it.</a></p>

<p><a data-t="430">Their favorite was one that was called head and shoulders, which was this</a> <a data-t="436">triple peak thing where the third rally of a stock failed and it ended up falling below its original</a> <a data-t="441">price. And so what you really wanted to be was where the smart money was, which was selling to</a> <a data-t="445">buyers at the top of the head there. As I say, the underlying theory here was a kind of psychology</a> <a data-t="452">of market segments, which is not an unreasonable idea. John Maynard Keynes made a lot of money in</a> <a data-t="457">the stock market in his day and once remarked that the business of the investor was to predict what</a> <a data-t="461">average opinion thought average opinion would be. It's a good idea. Men who,</a> <a data-t="483">as you can see here, looked at stock prices in the paper.</a></p>

<p><a data-t="483">Anyway, the chart men didn't think</a> <a data-t="489">these patterns were iron rules or laws of nature. They regularly cautioned that you can't just look</a> <a data-t="494">at the chart and read the future, obviously, because if you could then everybody would be</a> <a data-t="497">doing that. <p><a data-t="543">What killed this theory in the end was the rise of the efficient</a> <a data-t="548">markets hypothesis in its various forms, and that's the idea that in something like the stock</a> <a data-t="553">market, asset prices reflect all available information, or at least all publicly available</a> <a data-t="558">information about an asset. <h3>What makes visualization trustworthy?</h3>

<p><a data-t="592">The thing about the chart men is that there's nothing untrustworthy about their data.</a> <a data-t="597">The stream of information they used came straight from Dow Jones, and as for the score, well, the</a> <a data-t="602">thing about technical analysis is that it wasn't that technical. Really the only score or technique</a> <a data-t="606">they used was putting the numbers into graphs, which was a painstaking business. You had to do</a> <a data-t="610">it by hand, and there's nothing wrong with the graphs either, considered as graphs. Some of them</a> <a data-t="614">are really good. What's wrong, of course, is the story, which is nonsense.</a></p>

<p><a data-t="614">Now, we don't like to</a> <a data-t="622">think of ourselves as the sort of people who'd be taken in by something like looking for head</a> <a data-t="626">and shoulders patterns in price charts, and certainly we don't want to think of ourselves</a> <a data-t="630">as the sort of people who are inflicting some version of this sort of thing on our audiences.</a> <a data-t="634">For one thing, you might say, look, I'm not trying to predict the future. I just want to describe</a> <a data-t="639">something that happened. I just want to explore my data. Or you might say, hey, I actually have a</a> <a data-t="643">real theory, a proper theory that can explain the thing I'm showing you. I have real evidence.</a></p>

<p><a data-t="648">But this tendency really is pervasive. Here's an example from a few years ago. Vox published a</a> <a data-t="653">story discussing this startling rise in the availability of fats, especially vegetable fats,</a> <a data-t="658">in people's diets. It was pretty widely circulated. That word availability should alert you already to</a> <a data-t="665">that there might be something going on, because the availability of something in your diet isn't</a> <a data-t="668">the same as you actually consuming it. But beyond that, the fact that something like 20 pounds of</a> <a data-t="673">this increase happened in about a year is odd. You can probably guess how this ends,</a> <a data-t="680">but the thing is people really, and I mean really, wanted to tell a story about this trend.</a> <a data-t="685">Maybe something about long-term shifts in the American diet, the insidious rise of veganism,</a> <a data-t="692">stress eating caused by the Y2K problem, the emergence of avocado toast as a central feature</a> <a data-t="698">in millennials' lives. That was actually one of the theories. In fact, what happened was a change</a> <a data-t="705">in reporting requirements.</a></p>

<p><a data-t="705">A good rule of thumb is that if you see a sudden shift or discontinuity</a> <a data-t="709">in a time series like this, your first question should be, did something about the data generating</a> <a data-t="713">process change? You should sort of tap the instruments. Or better, try finding someone</a> <a data-t="717">who knows where the data came from. As Tom Smith of the National Opinion Research Center likes to</a> <a data-t="723">say, if you want to measure change, you can't change the measure. In this case, unlike with</a> <a data-t="728">the Chartman, there really is a big signal in the data. It's just not signaling what people imagined.</a> <a data-t="732">But in both cases, what people want to do is eyeball a graph and spin you a yarn.</a></p>

<p><a data-t="732">I don't think</a> <a data-t="740">graphs are uniquely to blame here. People like to tell stories, and they'll tell them with whatever</a> <a data-t="744">is at hand. But I think it's fair to say that people giving talks like the one I'm giving right</a> <a data-t="749">now can be tempted to make quite strong claims about visualization's unique capacity to provide</a> <a data-t="755">insights into the structure of your data. Maybe it's just the idea that visual storytelling or</a> <a data-t="760">a strong data narrative makes good graphics uniquely compelling in a way that conveys your</a> <a data-t="765">insights to others. And implicitly or explicitly, there's also this idea that the best data</a> <a data-t="770">visualizations are authoritative, that they can decisively settle a question or definitively make</a> <a data-t="775">a point. We kind of want these things to go together, right? We want to make something that</a> <a data-t="778">fuses an insightful, compelling, and authoritative use of data into a single, immediately accessible</a> <a data-t="783">image. Ideally something like this, for instance, the one John Snow made with the Broad Street water</a> <a data-t="788">pump, pinpointing the existence of a cholera outbreak before going and removing the handle</a> <a data-t="793">from the pump. The little squares are measures of deaths in the buildings on the streets.</a></p>

<p><a data-t="798">There really is something to the idea that a good graph can do a fantastic job here. A compelling</a> <a data-t="804">and authoritative insight is an epiphany, a moment of truth. And a great graph is a kind of portable</a> <a data-t="810">epiphany. The history of data visualization, or maybe I should say the history of data visualization</a> <a data-t="814">talks, gives many examples. These often come with little stories of their own, little tiny myths,</a> <a data-t="819">like how a particular image kick-started the field of epidemiology, or explained why the Challenger</a> <a data-t="824">space shuttle exploded, or summed up the reality of climate change for people. But I think we need</a> <a data-t="830">to be careful.</a></p>

<h3>What does it mean to trust a visualization?</h3>

<p><a data-t="1125">And there's that last part. We want our visualizations to be trustworthy in some</a> <a data-t="1129">general sense. Are they? Can they be? And what exactly is the role of software when it comes</a> <a data-t="1135">to making our work more trustworthy? After all, when someone tells you a story, when someone</a> <a data-t="1140">shows you a graph and gives you an interpretation of it, you want to know whether you can believe</a> <a data-t="1144">them, whether you can trust it. Here's why I think this is worth emphasizing. I think that whatever</a> <a data-t="1150">it is that makes a data visualization trustworthy, it isn't really something you can include in the</a> <a data-t="1154">graph. If you look at stock examples of bad graphs, especially older ones, it's kind of comforting</a> <a data-t="1160">because most of them look really janky. And the same goes for contemporary examples of bad</a> <a data-t="1165">marketing materials or from media outlets trying to spin some ridiculous visual summary of polling</a> <a data-t="1170">data. They don't look trustworthy. It's possible to make janky graphs with ggplot, but you kind of</a> <a data-t="1177">have to work at it. By default, ggplot makes a lot of good choices for you, and this gives it what</a> <a data-t="1183">you might call a kind of aura of plausibility. Whatever data you're showing and whatever story</a> <a data-t="1188">you're telling benefits from a kind of rhetoric of credibility that's built into the software,</a> <a data-t="1193">more or less out of the box. With just a little bit of extra work, you can polish up your dot plot</a> <a data-t="1198">to make a poster, or if it's your job, you can make an effective visual argument into financial</a> <a data-t="1204">times like John Byrne Murdoch does there, or a beautiful, well-thought-out contribution to</a> <a data-t="1208">Tidy Tuesday like Cedric Shearer does in the bottom right corner there. So what does that mean</a> <a data-t="1214">to trust a visualization?</a></p>

<p><a data-t="1214">As you might expect, there are large social scientific and philosophical</a> <a data-t="1220">literatures about trustworthiness. And like in many social scientific literatures, if you read them,</a> <a data-t="1224">you find that empirical studies confirm that trust has many dimensions. It's also really quite</a> <a data-t="1229">hard to measure, and it's very complicated. And like in many philosophical literatures, if you</a> <a data-t="1234">read them, it turns out that trust has many senses, and it's really quite hard to give a unified</a> <a data-t="1239">account of, and it's very complicated. Ever since the work of Annette Byer, the philosophical</a> <a data-t="1244">literature has made this distinction between trust and what gets called mere reliability.</a></p>

<p><a data-t="1248">Now, in ordinary speech, trust can mean reliability too, but the idea is that our</a> <a data-t="1252">everyday way of talking is fairly loose, and it runs together something that's worth making a</a> <a data-t="1257">distinction about. And the argument is roughly that there are a lot of things in people that</a> <a data-t="1260">we rely on without actually trusting them.
